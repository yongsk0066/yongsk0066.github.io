As shown in the video, the Raptor engine has already reached its third generation. Just from looking at the images, it's become remarkably simpler in appearance. Elon mentioned that while it looks simple on the outside, a great deal of actual simplification has gone into it as well.\
That much complexity is hidden beneath the surface. To achieve this, they removed the heat shield and integrated functions internally.

## Elon Musk's 5-Step Engineering Process

#### 1. Make the requirements less dumb
- Identify what the customer actually needs
- Especially when a smart person defined the requirement, there's a higher chance it hasn't been sufficiently validated.
- Analyze the root cause hidden behind the surface-level problem (e.g., using the 5 Whys technique)
#### 2. Delete the part or process
- There are always unnecessary parts or processes added "just in case" they might be needed.
- To quickly verify whether these ambiguous items are truly necessary, write the name of the person responsible — not the team name — so you can ask them directly.
#### 3. Simplify and optimize the design
- The most common mistake engineers make is spending time optimizing something that shouldn't exist in the first place.
- We've been trained throughout high school and university to always answer the question — but sometimes we need to question whether the premise of the question is even correct.
- Minimize if-else blocks to improve workflow and achieve faster results
#### 4. Accelerate cycle time
- But don't push for speed before completing the first three steps. Otherwise, you're just digging your own grave faster.
#### 5. Automate
- Automate the optimized process so that work happens without human intervention

Aerospace engineering and programming are different fields, but I believe Elon's engineering principles — coming from someone who was also a developer — are just as applicable to software engineers.\
Each point is independently important, but the order of these principles matters just as much.

When developing software or solving algorithm problems, I often struggle trying to create something perfect from the very start.
Looking back, I think there were a few reasons for this:
- Seeing already-completed code (libraries, previous codebases, etc.) created the illusion that such polished forms are born fully formed from the beginning
- Trying to write perfect code from the outset leads to spending infinite amounts of time

I believe that simple code contains far more thought and engineering than it appears.\
I can't say simplicity is always best, but I think such code doesn't just appear out of nowhere — it's born through a process of accumulated thought and time being compressed together.

Is complex code bad?\
I'd say it just hasn't gone through the process yet.

Complex code is not bad.\
At the very least, that's how we should think about it.
Rather than fixating on the current state of complexity, we should focus on the process of making things simpler.

Don't fear complexity — focus on the process, and enjoy it.
